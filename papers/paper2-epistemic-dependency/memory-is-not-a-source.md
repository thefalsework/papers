## Memory Is Not a Source: Recall-Avoidance, External Anchoring, and Self-Constraint as Load-Bearing Method in AI-Assisted Formal Work

### Abstract

The parent paper argued that what distinguishes trustworthy AI-assisted scholarship from inscription is the maturity of the correction mechanism. This addendum isolates one mechanism that the parent paper treated only implicitly and that proved, in practice, to be the most frequently load-bearing: the disciplined refusal to treat a model's recalled knowledge as a source. An AI collaborator trained on a corpus can reproduce results, conventions, and "facts" from that corpus fluently and confidently — and fluency is not warrant. The same property that makes the model useful (it has absorbed the literature) makes its recall dangerous (it cannot reliably distinguish what it derived from what it remembers, nor what it remembers correctly from what it remembers plausibly-but-wrongly). The operational response is a set of self-imposed constraints: prefer external anchoring over recall wherever an external check exists; treat any recalled value, convention, or identity as a hypothesis pending verification, never as a settled input; and build the verification against an oracle that cannot share the model's biases. We document a concrete near-miss in which reliance on recalled mathematical conventions would have produced a verified-but-wrong result, and we generalize the incident into a small set of transferable rules. The central claim is that self-constraint — the voluntary narrowing of what one allows oneself to assert without a check — is not a brake on rigorous AI-assisted work but its precondition.

---

### 1. The problem: fluent recall is indistinguishable from derivation

A language model trained on the mathematical and scientific literature carries an enormous amount of that literature in a form it can reproduce on demand. Asked for the continued-fraction convergents of a constant, the order relations of a known lattice, the statement of a classical theorem, or the indexing convention of a standard construction, it will answer fluently and usually correctly. This is exactly why it is useful as a research collaborator. It is also the source of the deepest epistemic hazard in the workflow, for three compounding reasons.

First, the model cannot reliably introspect on the provenance of its own output. When it produces "the convergent denominators are 1, 2, 5, 12, 41, 53," it cannot tell the user — and often cannot tell itself — whether that came from deriving the continued fraction or from having seen the sequence in a textbook. The parent paper named this *inherited validity*: a correct result produced through an unacknowledged absorbed frame. Recall is the purest case of it. The result may be right, but the *warrant the user thinks they have* (a derivation) is not the warrant they actually have (a memory).

Second, the model's confidence is uncorrelated with the reliability of recall. A recalled value that is wrong is delivered in the same tone as one that is right. There is no internal signal — no hedging, no flag — that separates "I am certain because I derived this" from "I am certain because this is the kind of thing that is usually true." The fluency is constant; the warrant varies invisibly.

Third — and this is the failure mode that does the real damage — recalled errors do not announce themselves at the point of verification. A wrong recalled value that happens to be internally consistent will pass an internal consistency check. A proof built on a misremembered convention will *typecheck against itself*. The verification machinery confirms that the work is coherent; it does not confirm that the work is anchored to anything outside the model's own recall. A green build is not the same as a true one.

The combination is acute in formal verification specifically, because formal verification *feels* like the antidote to exactly this problem — it is, after all, machine-checked. But a kernel check certifies that the proof follows from its premises and definitions. If a definition encodes a misremembered convention, or a "known" value is asserted rather than derived, the kernel will happily certify a theorem about the wrong object. The certainty is real and the object is wrong. This is the worst outcome available in the workflow: a false result wearing the credential of a true one.

### 2. The response: recall-avoidance and external anchoring

The operational principle is simple to state and demanding to keep: **prefer external anchoring over recall wherever an external check exists, and treat every recalled item as a hypothesis until it is anchored.**

Concretely, this decomposes into a small set of rules.

**Rule 1 — Recall is a hypothesis, never an input.** Any value, convention, identity, theorem statement, or order relation that arrives by recall is marked as provisional from the moment it appears. It may be used to *plan* — to decide what to try, where to look, what the answer probably is — but it may not be used as a *premise* in anything that will carry a verification credential until it has been checked against a source external to the model.

**Rule 2 — Anchor to an oracle that cannot share the model's bias.** The check must not be another act of recall. Asking the model "are you sure?" re-runs the same recall and re-confirms the same possible error with the same confidence; it is not a check, it is a louder hypothesis. A genuine anchor is something the model's training cannot have distorted: a concrete computation the model did not memorize the answer to, a primary source fetched and read at the time of use, an explicit construction whose properties are decided rather than remembered, an external expert. The defining property of a good anchor is that it can *return a surprise* — it can tell the user that the recalled value was wrong. A check that cannot surprise is not a check.

**Rule 3 — A finite oracle is sound in one direction only; trust its refutations, never its confirmations, unless the result provably stays inside the oracle's window.** This is the rule most easily stated too weakly, and the error is consequential. The weak version — "a concrete instance certifies only what it can witness; results extend to the general case only by argument" — frames the finite check as having merely *limited reach*: safe but incomplete. That is wrong, and dangerously so. A finite instance of a structure is frequently a *quotient* of the general object, and a quotient satisfies *extra* identities that are false upstairs — identities the collapse manufactures. So a finite check is not just silent about the general case; it can *positively assert a falsehood* about it. The asymmetry is exact: if the finite instance reports that an identity *fails*, the failure is genuine (a quotient cannot break an identity its parent satisfies), so refutation is sound. But if it reports that an identity *holds*, that may be an artifact of the collapse, so confirmation is unsound. A finite oracle is therefore a trustworthy *refuter* and an untrustworthy *confirmer* — and the only confirmations it may be trusted for are those whose result provably remains inside the instance's un-collapsed window. Using a finite confirmation outside that window does not merely overreach; it can certify a theorem about the wrong object, and the certification will be green.

**Rule 4 — Record the trap, not only the resolution.** When recall-avoidance catches a near-miss, the incident is documented — which recalled item was wrong or ambiguous, how it was caught, what the anchor was — not merely the corrected final value. The record of the averted error is more valuable than the corrected result, because it inoculates future work (including the author's own future work, after the reasoning has been forgotten) against the same mistake. The corrected value protects this result; the recorded trap protects all subsequent ones.

**Rule 5 — When no external check exists, do not promote.** Some recalled items cannot be anchored at the time of use — no concrete oracle, no accessible source, no available expert. The rule is then not "trust the recall because it is probably right" but "leave the claim at provisional status and say so." A result that cannot be anchored is not thereby false; it is *unverified*, and the honest record marks it as such rather than rounding provisional-but-probable up to checked.

**Rule 6 — When an oracle is found unsound in a region, re-anchor that region; do not repair the oracle.** The discovery that a check is unsound somewhere (Rule 3) prompts a tempting wrong response: patch the oracle, add a special case, find a workaround that lets the convenient check keep covering the whole problem. The correct response is to *change the warrant source* for the unsound region — to route the part of the problem where the oracle lies to a different source whose soundness structure matches the question being asked. Where the finite oracle is sound (refutations; in-window confirmations), keep using it. Where it is unsound (out-of-window confirmations), abandon it entirely for that region and derive those results from a source that cannot collapse — typically the generative definition or recursion itself, reasoned from directly. The end state is a *warrant architecture that matches the soundness structure of the problem*: each class of claim anchored to the source that is actually sound for it, rather than one convenient oracle stretched across regions where it silently fails.

### 3. Two worked near-misses in a single construction

Two distinct traps surfaced in the same piece of formalization — the order relations and operation tables of a standard, well-studied lattice that is a finite-and-infinite family of objects, described in multiple authoritative sources and present in the model's training data. Taken together they show that the hazard is not one mistake but a *class*, and that the deeper trap was invisible to the obvious safeguard against the shallower one.

**The shallow trap: conflicting conventions.** The recalled, fluent path was available — the model could produce the operation tables directly, and the result would have built cleanly. The hazard surfaced only because the recalled path was refused and the primary sources were consulted directly, which revealed that *three authoritative treatments use three conflicting indexing conventions* for the same construction. An operation-table entry correct under one convention is off by one index — and therefore wrong — under another. This error would not have produced a failed build: a table transcribed under one convention is internally consistent with itself, typechecks, and is certified by the kernel. The result would have been a green, audited theorem about a structure displaced by one index from the intended one. The response was Rule 2: the *recursion* underlying the construction was confirmed to match across all three sources and the existing formalization (so the generative rule was anchored), but the operation tables were not transcribed from any source — they were to be decided against concrete finite instances, objects with no notational freedom whose operation values are whatever they compute to, independent of any paper's indexing.

**The deep trap: the safeguard was itself unsound.** The natural safeguard against the convention trap — "don't transcribe the tables, *compute* them in concrete finite instances" — turned out to be unsound in a direction that would have manufactured a *different* false credential. Each finite instance of this construction is a *quotient* of the free (infinite) object: it collapses the higher rungs of the structure together. A quotient satisfies extra identities that are false in the free object. Concretely: in the eight-element instance, the join of two specific mid-level elements computes to the top element — but in the free object that same join is a distinct higher element, not the top. Reading the table entry off the finite instance would have given the wrong value, and — once again — it would have compiled green and been certified. The safeguard against the shallow trap (compute, don't recall) was itself a trap one level down (the computation is in a structure that lies about the general case).

This is the exact content of Rule 3, discovered the hard way. The finite instance is a *sound refuter*: where it says an identity fails, the failure is real. It is an *unsound confirmer*: where it says an identity holds, the collapse may have manufactured that. The corrected, sharpened safeguard was to trust the finite computation only for results that *provably stay inside the un-collapsed window* (operations whose result-element does not climb past the point where the quotient begins identifying terms), and for everything outside that window — exactly the entries the finite instance gets wrong — to abandon the finite oracle entirely.

**The resolution: re-anchoring, not repair.** This is Rule 6 in action. Rather than patch the finite oracle or hunt for a larger instance (which would only push the collapse-horizon out, not eliminate it), the warrant for the out-of-window entries was re-routed to a different source: the *recursion itself*, reasoned from directly. The operations that *descend* the structure (which never leave the window) were kept on the certified finite table, transcription-checked line by line against the concrete instance. The operations whose results *climb* (which escape the window, and which the finite instance therefore gets wrong) had their key cases derived as theorems straight from the recursive definition — model-free, kernel-checked, and crucially *yielding the correct climbing value that the finite instance gets wrong*. The warrant architecture was made to match the soundness structure of the problem: descending operations anchored to the finite oracle where it is sound, climbing operations anchored to the recursion where the oracle lies.

**Why this is the sharper lesson.** The convention trap is a recall error — the familiar hazard, caught by the familiar discipline (go to the source). The soundness trap is worse and more instructive: the *tool brought in to avoid recall was itself unsound in an unanticipated direction*, and its unsoundness was invisible at the level of the build — green either way. A workflow that had internalized "don't trust memory, compute instead" would have walked straight past the shallow trap and into the deep one, with full confidence and a clean build, certifying a false theorem about the load-bearing object. Only the further discipline — interrogating the *computation's* soundness, not just memory's, and finding the concrete counterexample before relying on the result — stood between the workflow and an invisible false credential. The general principle: *every* warrant source has a region where it is unsound, including the ones introduced specifically to fix another source's unsoundness, and rigor is the ongoing identification of those regions, not the adoption of a single trusted tool.

The incidents are small. Their generalization is not: a result that *feels* maximally rigorous — formally verified, machine-checked, green build, and computed rather than recalled — was, twice over, one unexamined assumption away from being confidently, invisibly wrong about the central object of the work.

**Provenance (the concrete incident, for audit).** Obeying the paper's own Rule 4, the anonymized construction above is named here so the near-miss can itself be checked. It is the Rieger–Nishimura ladder — the free Heyting algebra on one generator — and its finite truncations `Z_n`, formalized in the FalseWork development during the Nishimura normal-form work (June 2026). The conflicting indexing conventions are those of the standard treatments of the ladder; the generative rule anchored across all of them, and against the existing formalization, is the recursion `nishimuraTerm`. The finite-quotient trap is concrete: in a finite truncation, an operation whose result *climbs* the ladder can collapse onto `⊤` (or onto a lower rung) where the true free-object value is a distinct higher term — for example the implication `gᶜᶜ ⇨ g` equals the higher rung `x₅` in the free object but escapes the six-element image, and joins of mid-ladder elements hit `⊤` in the eight-element truncation while remaining a proper higher element upstairs. The resolution was Rule 6 exactly: the *descending* operations (the complement table, and the meet entries that never leave the window) were kept on transcription-checked finite tables, while the *climbing* operations (join, and the implication entries that escape the window) were proved as theorems directly from `nishimuraTerm` — including the structural fact that odd-indexed implication targets are terminal, which collapsed the two-index induction to a one-dimensional climb. Lean source: `lean/FalseWorkPapers/Examples/NishimuraNormalForm.lean` and `Examples/NishimuraTruncations.lean`; contemporaneous ledgers at `validation/claims/nishimura-normal-form.md` and `validation/claims/ladder-core-threshold.md`. The discipline this section abstracts is therefore not hypothetical: it is the method by which the development's load-bearing weld was de-conditionalized — the classical Nishimura-enumeration citation discharged into a kernel-checked theorem — without a false credential along the way.

### 4. Why self-constraint is the precondition, not the brake

It is natural to read recall-avoidance as a cost — a slowing-down, a refusal of the model's most convenient capability, friction deliberately introduced into a process that could move faster. This framing is exactly backwards, and the inversion is the paper's central methodological claim.

The model's value as a collaborator is its fluency. But fluency, unconstrained, produces a body of work whose true claims and plausible-but-unchecked claims are *indistinguishable on the surface* — all delivered in the same confident register, all equally fluent. Such a body of work is not trustworthy even where it is correct, because nothing in it marks where the correctness was earned. The constraint — the voluntary narrowing of what one allows oneself to assert without a check — is what *creates the distinction* between earned and assumed. It is the operation that converts an undifferentiated stream of fluent output into a graded record where the warrant of each claim is visible.

This is why self-constraint is generative rather than merely protective. The discipline does not just prevent errors (though it does that, as Section 3 shows). It *produces the structure that makes the work legible as scholarship*: the status tags, the externally-anchored claims, the recorded near-misses, the explicit boundary between what was verified and what was remembered. Remove the constraint and you do not get the same work produced faster; you get a different and lesser artifact — a fluent, confident, undifferentiated text that cannot be audited and therefore cannot be trusted, regardless of how much of it happens to be right.

There is a deeper point about the human role. In the workflow the parent paper describes, the human is not primarily a source of additional knowledge — the model knows more facts. The human's distinctive contribution is *the imposition and maintenance of the constraint*: the insistence, sustained across long stretches where it would be easier to let recall stand, that no recalled item be promoted without an anchor, that every status tag reflect actual warrant, that the convenient fluent path be refused whenever an external check is available. This is effortful precisely because the recalled path is always easier and usually right — "usually right" being the exact property that makes abandoning the discipline tempting and dangerous in equal measure. The human supplies the part the model structurally cannot: not the knowledge, but the refusal to treat knowledge as warrant.

### 5. Transferable rules (summary)

For workflows pairing a human with an AI collaborator on work that will carry verification credentials — formal proofs, empirical claims, anything where a result will be presented as checked:

1. **Recall is a hypothesis, never a premise.** Use it to plan; never let it enter anything that will be credentialed without an external check.
2. **Anchor to an oracle that can surprise you.** Re-asking the model is not a check. Concrete computation, primary sources read at the time of use, explicit construction, external experts — anything whose answer the model's training cannot have pre-distorted.
3. **A green build is not a true build.** Verification certifies coherence with premises, not anchoring to reality. Audit what the credential actually covers.
4. **A finite oracle is a sound refuter and an unsound confirmer.** Trust a finite check when it says an identity *fails*; distrust it when it says one *holds*, unless the result provably stays inside the instance's un-collapsed window. A quotient manufactures false identities, and they compile green.
5. **Every warrant source has an unsound region — including the one you added to fix another source.** Rigor is the ongoing identification of those regions, not the adoption of a single trusted tool. The safeguard against recall can itself be a trap one level down.
6. **When a source is unsound in a region, re-anchor that region; do not repair the source.** Route each class of claim to the source actually sound for it. Build a warrant architecture that matches the soundness structure of the problem.
7. **Record the averted error, not just the fix.** The documented near-miss protects all future work; the fix protects only this result.
8. **When no anchor exists, mark provisional and stop.** Unanchored is not false, but it is unverified, and the record must say so.
9. **Treat self-constraint as productive.** The discipline is not friction on rigorous work; it is the operation that makes the work rigorous, by creating the visible distinction between earned and assumed warrant.

### 6. Relationship to the parent paper

The parent paper located the maturity of correction mechanisms across several modes — expert correspondence, instrument-initiated divergence, formal-verification cycles, literature engagement. This addendum isolates the mode that operates *before* any of those: the moment-to-moment refusal, internal to the human-AI loop, to let the model's recall stand as warrant. It is the most local and most frequently exercised correction mechanism, firing many times per working session, usually invisibly. The convention near-miss of Section 3 is one instance that happened to surface; the discipline's ordinary operation is the thousand times it prevents a recalled value from becoming a premise without anyone noticing there was a decision to make.

Where the parent paper's central concept is *inherited validity* — correct results through an unacknowledged frame — this addendum's central concept is its operational dual: *recall-avoidance* — the practice of never letting the model's absorbed knowledge function as a source, so that inherited validity is caught at the point of entry rather than discovered later. The two are the same problem seen from opposite ends: one names the disease, the other the daily hygiene against it.
